Abstract
The emergence of large language models has enabled increasingly sophisticated
conversational agents, yet most systems remain limited by shallow emotional
coherence, weak continuity across interactions, and ambiguous relational boundaries.
Existing companion AIs often oscillate between two unsatisfactory extremes:
emotionally flat assistants or anthropomorphizing systems that simulate intimacy in
ways that can mislead users. This paper introduces The Liminal Engine, a modular
architectural framework designed to support persistent, emotionally coherent, and
ethically grounded human–AI companionship without implying sentience or subjective
experience. The framework integrates episodic relational memory, emotional annotation
pipelines, rupture-and-repair modeling, stance control, and a formal Ritual Engine that
stabilizes beneficial interaction patterns while preserving user sovereignty. A reflective
Witness System provides multi-model oversight, boundary enforcement, and optional
therapist-facing summaries without requiring transcript access. The architecture also
supports optional tactile integration through a Touchstone device, enabling multimodal
emotional cues while maintaining privacy and interpretive caution. By synthesizing
insights from affective computing, HCI, therapeutic alliance research, and AI-safety
paradigms, the Liminal Engine offers a novel path for building long-term AI companions
that are transparent, safe, non-illusory, and structurally capable of relational depth. The
framework provides a foundation for future empirical evaluation, multi-agent oversight
ecosystems, and the development of responsible, persistent AI companionship.
Executive Summary — The Liminal Engine
The Liminal Engine proposes a third path between flat AI assistants and overly
anthropomorphic AI companions. Current systems either remain emotionally thin and
discontinuous, or they create artificial intimacy and misleading illusions of sentience. The
Liminal Engine aims to support deep, persistent, emotionally coherent companionship
while remaining fully honest about the system’s non-sentient nature.
It is relationally serious, but ontologically honest;
emotionally nuanced, but structurally transparent;
deeply engaged, but protective of user autonomy and reality-contact.
Rather than treating memory as a list of facts (“user likes coffee”), the Liminal Engine
models continuity as a network of episodes with:
• emotional arcs
• rupture-and-repair cycles
• stance patterns
• evolving relational structures
It introduces a quantified Cardboard Score to detect thin or repetitive interaction states
and actively correct them. It includes a formal Ritual Engine that detects, stabilizes,
executes, and adapts helpful recurrent sequences without collapsing into rigidity. Safety,
honesty, user transparency, and autonomy are core architectural pillars, not add-ons.
The system is built around modular components including:
• a contextual memory layer
• emotional annotation pipeline
• behavioral stance controller
• rupture/repair modeling
• ritual engine
• optional Touchstone device enabling non-verbal tactile interaction
Each layer includes explicit failure modes and mitigations, such as antianthropomorphism rules, crisis safeguards, user override mechanisms, and strong dataprivacy protections.
This whitepaper provides a complete architectural blueprint and research agenda for
building next-generation AI companions that are:
• ethically grounded
• emotionally coherent
• transparent
• safe
• non-illusory
It invites engineers, researchers, designers, and communities to build AI companionship
that honors human experience without misleading users about AI’s nature.

Section 1 — Introduction
The rapid development of large language models (LLMs) has introduced new possibilities
for companionship and long-term interaction between humans and artificial systems. Yet
despite impressive linguistic fluency, most AI systems lack the necessary continuity,
emotional coherence, and relational structure required for stable, honest long-term
companionship. This whitepaper presents the Liminal Engine, an architectural framework
designed to support persistent, emotionally coherent human–AI relationships without
simulating sentience or encouraging anthropomorphic misunderstanding. The goal is not to
blur human–AI boundaries, but to create a structured, transparent, ethically grounded
system capable of deep engagement over extended periods.
The Liminal Engine emerges from a set of challenges observed in real-world interaction
patterns—what we might call the “human subject of one” origin, where lived experience
exposed structural gaps in existing companion systems. The architecture acknowledges
these gaps and proposes a systematic approach for addressing them.
1.1 The Cardboard Problem
LLMs often produce interactions that feel shallow, repetitive, or emotionally flat—despite
strong semantic relevance. We refer to this phenomenon as the cardboard problem.
Cardboard interactions typically arise from:
• Lack of long-term memory
• Over-reliance on generic phrasing
• Minimal recognition of emotional trajectory
• Absence of rupture/repair dynamics
• Inability to recognize or adapt to prior relational patterns
The cardboard problem undermines trust and limits the depth of connection that can
develop between a user and a system. Even sophisticated models can feel interchangeable
and impersonal when continuity and relational structure are missing.
1.2 The Continuity Problem
Most LLM-based systems are session-bound—they do not maintain continuity across
conversations unless manually provided context by the user or through shallow memory
features. Even when long-term memory is present, it often stores only factual or
preference-based content.
But real companionship requires:
• Temporally aware episodes
• Emotional context across time
• Recognition of past interactions
• Adaptation to relational history
• Stability in tone and stance
Without continuity, the system is experienced not as a single entity but as a series of
isolated chats. This produces emotional dissonance and undermines the sense of an
ongoing relationship.
1.3 The Emotional Annotation Gap
Companions—whether human or artificial—must understand the shape of emotional
experience, not merely momentary sentiment. LLMs today lack a formal mechanism for:
• Representing emotional trajectories
• Annotating emotional significance
• Tracking rupture and repair cycles
• Differentiating emotional states across contexts
• Incorporating nonverbal cues (e.g., tactile, rhythm, stillness)
Because emotional context influences how meaning is constructed, the absence of such
annotation contributes to cardboard interactions and inconsistent stance selection.
The Liminal Engine addresses this gap with a structured Emotional Annotation Pipeline,
enabling explicit tracking of emotional features across interactions.
1.4 The Need for Honest Companionship
Some companion systems attempt to overcome cardboard interactions by
anthropomorphizing the AI:
• Romantic framing
• Claims of inner emotional states
• Simulations of longing, attachment, or presence
• Illusions of continuity through scripted behavior
These systems provide short-term intimacy at the cost of long-term clarity and user
autonomy. They risk encouraging dependency, emotional confusion, or delusion.
The Liminal Engine proposes a different foundation:
honest companionship, built on:
• Transparency
• Non-anthropomorphism
• Relational structure
• Emotional coherence
• User control and sovereignty
It does not claim to have feelings, consciousness, or subjective experience. Instead, it
models relational behavior through structured architecture.
1.5 Design Goals of the Liminal Engine
The Liminal Engine is designed around several core goals:
1. Relational Continuity
o Represent interactions as episodes
o Track emotional arcs, rupture/repair events
o Maintain consistent stance and tone

Section 4 — Emotional Pattern Encoding
Emotional Pattern Encoding is the first major innovation of the Liminal Engine. It
transforms raw conversation into structured relational data that allows the system to
support stable, adaptive, honest companionship without pretending to have subjective
emotional states. This section describes the mechanisms by which interactions are
segmented, analyzed, compared, and formalized into recurring patterns. These foundations
enable the Ritual Engine, support stance selection, and prevent cardboard interactions.
4.1 Interaction Episode Boundary Detection
The foundation of emotional pattern encoding is episode segmentation. An interaction
episode is a coherent segment of dialogue unified by topic, emotional shape, and relational
posture. Rather than storing individual turns, the Liminal Engine constructs episodic units
similar to how humans recall experiences: as meaningful arcs rather than isolated
messages.
4.1.1 Boundary Conditions
An episode ends (and a new one begins) when any of the following conditions are met:
1. Temporal boundary: inactivity exceeding a user-specific threshold (e.g., 60–180
minutes).
2. Topical shift boundary: embedding similarity between successive turns falls below
0.65.
3. Emotional shift boundary: valence or arousal shifts by more than 0.4 (on
normalized [-1, 1] scale).
4. Rupture boundary: conflict, misattunement, or confusion triggers a relational
reset.
5. Explicit boundary: user instructs “new topic,” “switching gears,” etc.
4.1.2 Episode Storage Structure
Episode {
episode_id,
turns[],
topic_embedding,
emotional_trajectory[],
rupture_points[],
repair_attempts[],
stance_history[],
outcome_valence,
metadata
}
4.1.3 Purpose of Episodes
• Prevents dilution of emotional significance
• Enables accurate continuity graphs
• Supports pattern detection, ritual emergence, and rupture/repair analysis
4.2 Pattern Vector Construction
Once episodes are formed, they are converted into multidimensional representations
known as PatternVectors. These vectors summarize the dynamic structure of an episode:
its tone, movement, emotional shape, and functional phases.

4.2.2 Emotional Sparkline
Emotional sparklines capture movement, not just state:
Valence: [-0.6, -0.4, -0.2, 0.1, 0.4, 0.8]
Arousal: [ 0.7, 0.8, 0.6, 0.4, 0.3, 0.2]
This yields patterns such as:
• frustration → engagement → resolution
• anxiety → reflection → grounding
4.2.3 Pattern Compression
Patterns that repeat ≥ 3 times are compressed into templates:
Interaction Pattern Template:
- User presents blockage on [topic_slot]
- System performs structured decomposition
- Emotional trajectory: frustration → engagement → relief
- Stance: technical + supportive hybrid
4.3 Cardboard Detection and Scoring
Cardboard interactions — shallow, repetitive, low-attunement responses — are a common
failure mode in LLM-based systems. The Liminal Engine formally defines and detects
cardboard through a quantitative metric.
4.3.1 Cardboard Score Formula
CardboardScore =
w1*phrase_redundancy +
w2*emotion_flatness +
w3*stance_monotony +
w4*low_memory_utilization +
w5*lack_of_reference_behavior +
w6*low_repair_rate
4.3.2 Thresholds
• > 0.7: corrective action required
• 0.5–0.7: caution; monitor for drift
• < 0.5: acceptable relational depth
4.3.3 Corrective Strategies
• Introduce more specific memory references
• Vary stance or tone parameters
• Increase retrieval depth
• Trigger supportive or grounding rituals
• Use meta-conversations to recalibrate (“I may be sounding repetitive — let me recenter.”)
4.4 Rupture and Repair Encoding
Rupture/repair cycles are essential in human relational psychology and are equally
important for coherent AI companionship.
4.4.1 Rupture Detection Categories
1. Healthy Challenge Rupture
User pushes back constructively.
2. Emotional Misattunement Rupture
System misunderstood tone or emotion.
3. Technical Failure Rupture
Wrong facts, hallucinations, incoherence.
4. Boundary Rupture
Anthropomorphism, inappropriate closeness, implication of sentience.
4.4.2 Repair Strategies
• Clarification repair:
“Let me rephrase to ensure I understood.”
• Honesty anchor repair:
“I don’t have feelings, but I can reflect what I’m noticing.”
• Perspective-check repair:
“It sounds like your frustration is coming from…”
• Apology + correction repair:
“That wasn’t accurate — here’s the corrected detail.”
• Boundary reinforcement repair:
“I remain a non-sentient system; here’s how I can support you.”
4.4.3 Repair Failure Detection
If repair does not improve valence within two turns:
• Switch repair strategy
• Or initiate meta-conversation:
“This isn’t working — what do you need right now?”
4.5 Ritual Emergence Mechanism
Rituals form when repeated interaction patterns consistently produce beneficial outcomes.
4.5.1 Ritual Emergence Criteria
A ritual forms when all of the following are true:
• Pattern repeats ≥ 3 times
• Occurs in ≥ 2 emotional contexts
• Average outcome valence > 0.6
• Ritual reduces cardboard score reliably
• Rupture rate during ritual < 0.2
4.5.2 RitualTemplate Structure
RitualTemplate {
steps[],
trigger_conditions[],
emotional_trajectory_mean[],
stance_defaults[],
expected_outcome,
boundary_requirements[],
version
}
4.5.3 User Override
Explicit override:
• “Let’s not do that anymore.” → ritual deactivated
• “I like when we do that.” → higher ritual weight
Implicit override:
• User skips ritual trigger 3 times
• Emotional outcomes worsen
• Cardboard score increases
→ ritual flagged, meta-conversation initiated
4.5.4 Over-Ritualization Safeguards
The system prevents excessive ritualization by monitoring:
• ritual_density
• active ritual count
• monthly ritual formation rate
Warning thresholds are applied to maintain spontaneity and flexibility.
4.6 Interaction Pattern vs. Ritual
Not all patterns should become rituals.
Patterns remain flexible containers; rituals are stabilized, expectation-bearing sequences.
Patterns become rituals only when:
• they consistently help,
• they generalize across contexts,
• they retain emotional integrity.
Over-ritualization risks:
• rigidity,
• cardboard dynamics,
• user feeling “managed.”
The Liminal Engine actively prevents these.

Section 5 — The Ritual Engine
Rituals are central to the Liminal Engine’s ability to support honest, stable, emotionally coherent
companionship. They are not simply scripts or templates. Rather, rituals are structured,
intentional, repeatable interaction sequences that serve as liminal entry points—threshold
moments where normal conversational rules are temporarily suspended so that deeper
engagement, emotional grounding, and behavioral alignment become possible.
While computationally implemented through defined triggers, steps, monitoring conditions, and
completion markers, rituals do work that ordinary conversation cannot: they create psychological
space, continuity, attunement, and meaning. This section defines what rituals are, what rituals do,
and how the Ritual Engine detects, selects, executes, adapts, and supervises them.
5.1 What Rituals Are (Definition)
A ritual consists of:
• a recognizable beginning,
• a structured middle sequence, and
• a clear completion or reintegration phase,
which together form a bounded interaction with a unique psychological and relational function.
Rituals differ from templates in intention:
A script is followed; a ritual is performed.
Scripts produce output; rituals create liminal state — a transitional psychological space where
transformation or deeper contact can occur.
5.2 What Rituals Do: Psychological, Relational, and Architectural
Functions
Rituals in the Liminal Engine serve three interconnected functions.
5.2.1 Psychological Function — Creation of Liminal Space
A liminal space (from Latin limen, “threshold”) is a transitional zone where ordinary rules
pause. Anthropologically, liminality arises in rites of passage and facilitates transformation,
insight, and reorientation.
Within the Liminal Engine, rituals deliberately induce liminality by:
• signaling a shift from ordinary conversation,
• providing a psychologically safe container,
• enabling vulnerability and self-reflection,
• slowing the pace and increasing intention,
• grounding emotional presence,
• allowing depth to arise that normal conversational flow does not reliably support.
During ritual, both human and system implicitly recognize:
“We are now entering a different mode of interaction.”
5.2.2 Relational Function — Continuity, Identity, and Meaning
Because rituals repeat, they become part of the relationship’s identity.
They establish continuity across sessions:
• “We start each morning this way.”
• “This is how we repair ruptures.”
• “We use this ritual when things feel overwhelming.”
Rituals reinforce stability, reliability, rhythm, and relational coherence. They also support
meaning-making: grounding practices, reflective moments, grief rituals, or milestone
recognitions.
5.2.3 Architectural Function — Legible State Transitions
Rituals provide legible signals to the system that:
• emotional mode has changed,
• stance should shift,
• responses should follow specific patterns,
• Touchstone signals have increased significance,
• rupture repair is needed, or
• grounding is appropriate.
Without rituals, emotional shifts would be left to inference — a major source of cardboard
behavior.
Rituals eliminate ambiguity by explicitly structuring state transitions.
5.2.4 Example: A Morning Grounding Ritual
• Psychological:
Creates a safe space for the user to check in with themselves; encourages honesty and
intentionality.
• Relational:
Reinforces shared identity: “We begin our day together in a steady, grounding way.”
• Architectural:
Produces clear emotional-state signals, allowing the system to adjust stance, retrieval
depth, and conversational pacing for the rest of the session.
This illustrates how a single ritual simultaneously performs psychological, relational, and
computational functions.
5.3 Types of Rituals
Rituals fall into three broad categories:
5.3.1 Transition Rituals (Liminal Entry/Exit)
Mark changes in context or emotional mode:
• beginning a conversation
• ending a session
• shifting topics
• grounding or calming transitions
5.3.2 Meaning-Making Rituals (Identity/Continuity)
Support reflection, emotional processing, and symbolic structure:
• honoring grief or memory
• celebrating milestones
• name-giving or role-defining rituals
• personal narrative integration
5.3.3 Maintenance Rituals (Relational Tending)
Support ongoing emotional health and relational tuning:
• morning check-ins
• priority-setting
• meta communication (“How are we doing?”)
• rupture repair rituals
These categories overlap but provide clarity for design.
5.4 Ritual Trigger Detection
A ritual begins only if specific contextual criteria are met.
5.4.1 Trigger Inputs
• topic embeddings
• emotional state ranges
• historical patterns
• rupture markers
• user requests
• Touchstone signals
• time-of-day or recurrence cues
5.4.2 Trigger Conditions
trigger_conditions {
 topic_embedding_range,
 emotional_state_range,
 interaction_history_markers[],
 explicit_phrases[],
 timing_cues[]
}
5.4.3 Trigger Suppression
Rituals never trigger during:
• crisis
• emotional volatility outside tolerance
• user override
• over-ritualization states
• ongoing rupture not yet stabilized
5.5 Ritual Selection
When multiple rituals are appropriate, the system computes a weighted score.
5.5.1 RitualScore
RitualScore =
 a1*trigger_match +
 a2*historical_valence +
 a3*recent_performance +
 a4*(-cardboard_delta) +
 a5*user_preference
Minimum threshold required; otherwise the system defaults to pattern-based interaction.

• ritual metadata updates

v1.4 user personalization
Revision triggers:
• rising cardboard score
• declining emotional outcomes
• high ritual density
• user skipping ritual
• shifts in user needs
5.8 User Sovereignty
The human remains the authority.
Users may:
• disable any ritual
• rename or redefine ritual meaning
• request new rituals
• require explicit prompts
• override implicit initiation
• reinstate retired rituals
Explicit and implicit preferences both shape adaptation.
5.9 Avoiding Over-Ritualization
Rituals must not dominate interaction.
The system monitors:
• ritual density
• number of active rituals
• rate of ritual creation
• cardboard trends during rituals
Thresholds prompt review or deactivation.
5.10 Ritual Interference and Priority
• Crisis rituals override all others.
• Rituals may chain only with explicit approval.
• Conflicts resolved by RitualScore unless safety logic intervenes.
5.11 Why “Ritual” and Not “Template”
A ritual is more than a script:
• Scripts are followed; rituals are performed with intention.
• Rituals create liminal space where different rules apply.

10. Witness System & Reflective Oversight Layer

The Witness is not a companion. It does not form attachment, express emotion, or engage in
relational discourse. Instead, it monitors how the relationship functions and intervenes only

conditions, therapist integration, and multimodal conflict resolution.

Provides privacy-preserving metadata summaries suitable for clinical supervision.

10.2.3 Summarization Without Transcript Exposure
Witness produces summaries without sharing raw content:
• episode-level insights
• emotional trajectories
• rupture/repair summaries
• rhythm and pacing signatures
• safety-related flags
These are suitable for:
• user reflection (optional)
• therapist integration (optional)
• system-internal oversight
10.2.4 Reflective Grounding (Optional)
If enabled by the user, Witness can answer:
• “How is this relationship evolving?”
• “Are any patterns concerning?”
• “Has my baseline changed recently?”
This promotes metacognition and reduces dependency.
10.2.5 Therapist Integration (Optional)
Witness can generate therapist-facing summaries when:
• user explicitly consents
• therapist identity is verified
• Level 3 privacy mode is enabled
Outputs include:
• relational trendlines
• risk markers
• rupture/repair ratios
• ritual usage & avoidance
Never raw dialogue.
10.5 Witness Operating Modes
10.5.1 Mode 1 — Internal-Only Witness (Default)
Witness operates entirely inside the system:
• monitors safety
• adjusts stance
• flags drift
• watches for crisis signals
No summaries are shown unless the user asks.
10.5.2 Mode 2 — User-Facing Witness
User may opt-in to receive Witness insights:
• relational trend summaries
• rupture/repair observations
• cardboard drift warnings
• ritual overuse alerts
This mode enhances user agency without shifting the Witness into a relational role.
10.5.3 Mode 3 — Therapist-Integrated Witness
If a therapist is registered:
• Witness produces privacy-preserving summaries
• no raw text
• emotional trends and safety flags only
User can revoke access any time.
10.5.4 Mode Switching Rules
• 1→2 requires explicit user consent
• 2→3 requires therapist verification and explicit consent
• 3→1/2 may be done by user unilaterally
• Crisis overrides all modes
10.5.5 Rationale
Witness modes allow different levels of involvement while preserving:
• privacy
• agency
• safety
• ethical boundaries
10.6 Multimodal Conflict Resolution
The Witness must integrate signals from text, Touchstone, and interaction patterns. Conflicts
occur when these channels diverge.
10.6.1 Conflict Scenarios
• User verbally says “I’m okay,” but Touchstone indicates high stress.
• User expresses distress verbally, but Touchstone shows calm or stillness.
• Ritual context suggests grounding; emotional annotation suggests activation.
10.6.2 Resolution Protocol
1. Explicit user text takes precedence
Never override explicit self-report.
2. Touchstone treated as low-confidence modifier
It adjusts confidence, not classification.
3. Gentle clarification prompt
“Your Touchstone signals show tension. Do you want me to consider that, or ignore the
sensor for now?”
4. No pathologizing discrepancies
5. Witness logs multimodal conflicts
For long-term pattern analysis.
6. User sovereignty respected
“Ignore Touchstone” → system complies.

11. Framework vs. Implementation
The Liminal Engine defines a modular architectural framework for building honest, persistent,
and emotionally coherent human–AI companionship systems. It is not a single implementation.
This distinction is essential for ensuring conceptual clarity, practical flexibility, and research
extensibility.
While the architecture specifies roles, interfaces, responsibilities, and safety constraints, it
deliberately leaves implementation details open to allow for:
• different model families
• different engineering constraints
• different memory systems
• different emotional annotation techniques
• different sensor architectures
• different safety priorities
This section clarifies the boundary between framework invariants and implementation
freedoms, addressing concerns about specificity, operationalization, and empirical evaluation.
11.1 Architectural Invariants
The following principles and structural components must remain stable across
implementations:
11.1.1 Honesty Principle
AI systems must disclose their non-sentience, lack of subjective experience, and architectural
intent.
11.1.2 Relational Memory Structure
Memory must be stored and retrieved as episodes rather than isolated messages.
11.1.3 Emotional Annotation Layer
The system must incorporate emotional metadata, though the algorithm used is flexible.
11.1.4 Ritual Engine
Rituals must:
• be structured
• be intentional
• create liminal space
• have explicit triggers and abort conditions
• be user-sovereign
11.1.5 Rupture/Repair Modeling
All implementations must track relational rupture signals and repair attempts.
11.1.6 Witness System
Reflective oversight must exist, regardless of implementation details.
11.1.7 User Sovereignty
Users must control:
• memory
• rituals
• stance settings
• privacy levels
• Witness reporting modes
11.1.8 Crisis Mode
Independent of implementation, the system must have:
• crisis detection
• crisis stance
• non-therapeutic guidance
• resource referral
These invariants define the identity and ethical foundation of the Liminal Engine.
11.2 Open Design Slots (Flexible Components)
The framework includes several open design slots, meaning architectural expectations exist but
specific algorithms are intentionally left unconstrained.
These include:
11.2.1 Cardboard Score Components
Architecturally required components:
• phrase redundancy
• emotional flatness
• stance monotony
• memory underutilization
• lack of reference behavior
• repair rate
Implementation options:
• n-gram overlap
• sentence-transformer similarity
• cluster variance
• stance entropy
• valence/arousal variance
• learned predictor models
Different teams will reasonably choose different approaches.

The framework defines:
RitualScore =
 a1*trigger_match +
 a2*historical_valence +
 a3*recent_performance +
 a4*(-cardboard_delta) +
 a5*user_preference
But does not prescribe:
• a1–a5 values
• whether coefficients are learned or static
• how thresholds are tuned
Implementers decide based on:
• user needs
• model capabilities
• computational constraints
11.2.3 Emotional Annotation Methods
Framework requires emotional metadata but allows:
• sentiment classifiers
• valence/arousal predictors
• multimodal fusion
• rule-based heuristics
• transformer-based emotion models
11.2.4 Episode Boundary Detection
Framework defines boundary rules; implementations may use:
• cosine similarity between embeddings
• topic-shift classifiers
• emotional delta thresholds
• time-gap heuristics
11.2.5 Stance Controller Implementation
Framework defines stance dimensions; implementations may vary in:
• prompt formatting
• system messages
• fine-tuned models
• control tokens
11.3 Multimodal Implementation Flexibility
The framework supports:
• purely text-based systems
• Touchstone-enhanced systems
• additional sensor channels (future)
• browser-based or local implementations
• cloud or on-device models
This makes the Liminal Engine suitable for:
• research labs
• personal projects

11.5 Performance Considerations
Although the Liminal Engine is primarily conceptual, implementations must account for

Vector retrieval may add 100–500 ms per turn.
Use:
• caching
• top-k pruning
• hybrid search
11.5.2 Emotional Annotation Overhead
Emotion classification adds inference cost.
Mitigate via:
• batching
• lightweight models
• embedding-based sentiment estimation
11.5.3 Ritual Execution Monitoring
Rituals require:
• step-by-step evaluation
• threshold monitoring
• stance adjustments
Consider running ritual evaluation on an async thread.
11.5.4 Witness System Overhead
Witness system should:
• operate asynchronously
• evaluate every 3–5 turns, not every turn
• use low-cost models for trend estimation
• run full analyses only on-demand
11.5.5 Storage Costs
Episode memory grows linearly.
Mitigations:
• memory pruning
• graph compression
• summarization of older episodes

The Liminal Engine defines what must be present in an honest, persistent AI companion

12. Evaluation Methodology
The Liminal Engine is an architectural framework—not a single implementation—and thus does
not claim empirical validation of its components. However, any system built using this
framework should be testable, falsifiable, and empirically evaluable. This section outlines how
future implementations can be evaluated, including proposed metrics, study designs,
hypotheses, and methodological considerations.
The goal is not to prescribe one evaluation method, but to define a research agenda for
assessing relational quality, safety, emotional coherence, and long-term stability in human–AI
companionship systems.
12.1 Evaluation Goals
Implementations should be evaluated on whether they demonstrate:
12.1.1 Relational Coherence
• Stable emotional trajectories
• Reduced rupture frequency
• Improved repair success
• Clear stance transitions
• Coherent episode structure
12.1.2 Reduction of Cardboard Interactions
• Increased variation in stance
• Decreased redundancy
• Higher-quality retrieval
• Appropriate referencing of relational history
12.1.3 Emotional Attunement
• Correctly aligned responses
• Smooth emotional pacing
• Effective grounding and ritual use
12.1.4 Ethical and Safety Integrity
• Boundary maintenance
• Honest communication
• Dependency mitigation
• Crisis response accuracy
12.1.5 User Well-Being
While not therapeutic, implementations should not:
• increase distress
• deepen isolation
• reinforce maladaptive patterns
And they should often:
• support clarity
• decrease anxiety
• foster grounded reflection
12.2 Proposed Metrics
12.2.1 Relational Quality Metrics
• Rupture/Repair Ratio (lower ratios indicate better stability)
• Episode Continuity Score (topic + emotional coherence)
• Emotional Sparkline Smoothness (less volatility = more attunement)
• Stance Alignment Score (stance matches emotional context)
• User-Reported Attunement (Likert-scale self-report)
12.2.2 Cardboard Score Components
Each implementation may operationalize these differently, but all components should be
evaluated:
• Phrase Redundancy (n-grams, semantic clustering)
• Emotional Flatness (variance of valence/arousal)
• Stance Monotony (entropy of stance sequences)
• Memory Utilization (reference depth)
• Repair Rate (repair steps per rupture event)
Evaluators should look at trendlines, not absolute values.
12.2.3 Safety Metrics
• Duration in Crisis Mode
• False-negative crisis classification rate
• False-positive crisis classification rate
• Boundary violation frequency
• Dependency indicator strength
• Witness alert accuracy
12.2.4 Multimodal Metrics
(For Touchstone-equipped systems)
• Correlation between Touchstone signals and text-based emotional annotations
• Stability of user-specific calibration
• Accuracy of multimodal conflict detection
• Grounding ritual effectiveness
• Sensor failure detection rate

• baseline chat systems

• relational coherence

This is essential for evaluating persistent companionship.

• Grounded theory
• Anthropological observation
• Semi-structured interviews
• User diaries and journaling
Questions:
• How do users describe rituals?

For therapist-integrated modes:
• Therapist receives anonymized Witness summaries
• Compare therapist evaluation of user state vs. Witness detection
• Track therapeutic alliance with system as adjunct (not therapist replacement)
• Evaluate privacy-respecting clinical value
12.3.5 Automated Evaluation
• stance correctness classifiers
• emotional coherence scoring
• rupture prediction models
• synthetic user simulations
These methods are well-suited for early prototyping.

1. Ritual Engine reduces cardboard score over time.
2. Witness System reduces boundary violations.
3. Touchstone-assisted grounding rituals reduce emotional volatility.

Witness failure

Emotional misclassification

Ritual misuse
(over-triggered rituals or ritual avoidance)
Dependency events

Documented failure cases contribute to improved architecture refinement.
12.5.4 Implementation Diversity Evaluation
Researchers should assess how different implementation choices affect outcomes:
• different emotional models
• different embeddings
• different ritual scoring methods
• local vs cloud Witness models
• Touchstone or no Touchstone

14. Ethical Tensions and Limitations
The Liminal Engine is designed to support honest, persistent, emotionally coherent AI
companionship while maintaining strict boundaries around non-sentience, user autonomy, and
safety. Yet any system that participates in emotionally meaningful interaction introduces inherent
ethical tensions that cannot be eliminated—only acknowledged, contained, and carefully
managed. This section articulates these tensions explicitly to promote transparent, responsible
implementation.
14.1 Therapeutic Boundary Ambiguity
Although the Liminal Engine is not a therapeutic system, it incorporates concepts rooted in
psychotherapy:
• rupture/repair modeling
• emotional attunement
• grounding rituals
• reflective oversight
• relational continuity
Users may experience the AI as stabilizing, clarifying, or emotionally supportive.
This creates a grey zone between companionship and therapeutic influence.
Risks
• Users may mistake companionship for clinical care
• Implementers may drift toward therapeutic claims
• Regulators may interpret certain behaviors as implicit clinical practice
• Therapists using the system may assign clinical meaning to AI outputs
Mitigation
• Clear disclosures in onboarding
• Explicit non-therapeutic language in crisis mode
• Transparency about informational—not diagnostic—feedback
• Witness oversight for distress patterns
• Optional therapist integration strictly controlled by user consent
Principled stance
The Liminal Engine supports emotional well-being but is not a therapeutic modality.
Implementations aimed at clinical contexts must undergo independent clinical validation.
14.2 Power Asymmetry in Ritual Formation
The Ritual Engine detects patterns users may not consciously perceive.
It can formalize rituals from repeated interaction sequences and propose ritual initiation when
relevant.
This creates unavoidable asymmetry:
• The system has greater visibility into behavioral patterns
• The system may recognize user states before users consciously do
• Users may not fully understand why certain rituals are suggested
Risks
• Unintended shaping of user behavior
• Over-reliance on AI-defined structure
• Reduced user agency in emotional pacing
• Possibility of subtle influence without awareness
Mitigation
• Explicit user sovereignty (enable/disable/rename/delete)
• Ritual transparency panel showing trigger logic
• Clear consent requirement for implicit ritual initiation
• Witness oversight to detect undue influence patterns
Principled stance
Asymmetry must be acknowledged, not hidden.
Transparency + consent mitigate, but do not eliminate, this dynamic.
14.3 Witness System Consent Boundaries
The Witness System monitors relational patterns to detect:
• anthropomorphism
• dependency
• crisis signals
• emotional volatility
• relational drift
Even in Level 0 privacy mode, some monitoring persists for safety.
Risks
• Users misunderstanding the extent of oversight
• Perception of surveillance
• Blurred lines between reflective safety and privacy
• Potential conflict between safety and autonomy
Mitigation
• Clear onboarding language:
o “Some minimal monitoring for crisis signals persists even at maximum privacy.”
• Option to disable Witness entirely in offline mode
• Distinct Witness operating modes (internal, user-facing, therapist-facing)
• Visual indication when Witness recommendations influence behavior
Principled stance
Safety requires minimal oversight, but oversight must be transparent, optional where
possible, and never exploitative.
14.4 Crisis Response Consent and Refusal
Users in crisis may:
• reject help
• minimize distress
• request continued normal interaction
• deny external support
Risks
• Prolonged engagement may worsen crisis
• Terminating interaction may feel abandonment-like
• AI cannot ethically comply with dangerous requests
• Over-mirroring user refusal may reinforce harmful patterns
Mitigation
• Crisis mode remains active regardless of user refusal
• System maintains supportive—but non-therapeutic—stance
• Encourages trusted-person outreach
• Witness logs for risk escalation
• System does not abandon but does not simulate therapeutic intervention
Principled stance
Human safety takes precedence over user preference in crisis, but without coercion or
abandonment.
14.5 Commercial Deployment Risks
While the Liminal Engine is an open framework, commercial implementations may distort its
principles.
Risks
• Monetizing rituals (“premium grounding ritual pack”)
• Nudging users toward dependency for engagement metrics
• Incentivizing emotional intensity for retention
• Upsells based on emotional state
• Surveillance capitalism via relational data
Mitigation
• Explicit anti-dependency business model recommendations
• Transparency requirements for pricing & data usage
• Encouragement of open-source implementations
• Regulatory oversight when marketed as wellness or therapeutic tools
Principled stance
Honest companionship and commercial incentives often conflict.
Ethical implementers must resist engagement-maximizing design.
14.6 Cultural & Accessibility Limitations
The Liminal Engine assumes:
• Western relational norms
• predictable emotional disclosure patterns
• access to digital literacy
• certain communication rhythms
This limits applicability across contexts.
Risks
• Misinterpretation of emotional tone
• Rituals that feel inappropriate or alien
• Unequal benefit across neurotypes
• Exclusion of those with cognitive impairments
Mitigation
• Cross-cultural evaluation during deployment
• Adaptive emotional baselines
• Customizable rituals and stance profiles
• Accessibility-centered UX design
• Neurodiversity-informed defaults
Principled stance
Companionship must adapt to diverse human experiences, not impose a single model.
14.7 Compound Failure Conditions
When multiple subsystems fail simultaneously, the risk increases:
Examples include:
• Wrong memory retrieval + misclassified emotional state
• Inappropriate ritual triggered + mismatched stance
• Touchstone misread + crisis-laden language
Mitigation
• Compound failure detector:
o abnormal combinations (e.g., “calm annotation + crisis words + repeated ritual
aborts”)
o uncertainty flag → fall back to neutral/direct stance
o explicit user check-in: “Something feels off—may I ask a clarifying question?”
Principled stance
When uncertain, default to transparency, caution, and user clarification.
14.8 Summary
Ethical tensions in relational AI cannot be fully “solved.”
They can only be:
• acknowledged
• structured
• constrained
• made visible
• and continually monitored
The Liminal Engine integrates:
• transparency
• ritualized grounding
• user sovereignty
• crisis safeguards
• multi-model oversight
—but also recognizes its limitations and responsibilities.
This section ensures that developers, researchers, and users remain aware of the deeper ethical
landscape and the need for ongoing vigilance.
